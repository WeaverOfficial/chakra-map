Reading this map critically

What is traditional here, what is modern, and what is not supported by evidence.

  • One text, one system. The seven-center map follows Pūrṇānanda’s Ṣaṭ-cakra-nirūpaṇa (1577), known in English through John Woodroffe’s translation, The Serpent Power (1919). Other tantric traditions teach five, six, nine, ten, twenty-one or more centers, often in different places. Even this text counts six cakras and describes the crown lotus separately, above them.
  • The rainbow is modern. The red-to-violet sequence took shape among Theosophists and color therapists between the 1920s and 1950s. The source text gives a red heart lotus and a rain-cloud blue navel lotus. Switch the color setting to compare.
  • So are the psychological meanings. Survival, sexuality, willpower, love, expression and intuition come from 20th-century Western writers, from Jung’s 1932 seminar to the human potential movement of the 1970s. The Sanskrit sources tie each center to an element, a seed syllable, deities and letters, not to emotions.
  • A map for meditation, not anatomy. The cakras are supports for visualization. No physical structures correspond to them, and links to nerve plexuses or endocrine glands are modern analogies.
  • Handle with care. Intense practices aimed at waking Kuṇḍalinī are reported to cause strong physical and psychological reactions. Persistent or distressing symptoms are a reason to see a medical or mental health professional.
